# Constellation lore, one entry per IAU constellation (88): Abbr|Figure|Origin|Tradition|Story
#
#   Abbr       IAU three-letter abbreviation, as in constellations.txt (Serpens' two figures share "Ser").
#   Figure     what the constellation depicts, in English ("the Hunter").
#   Origin     "ancient" (Ptolemy's list of 48, with the three parts of Argo Navis) or "modern" (devised
#              in the 16th to 18th centuries; their story is a history of the name, not a myth).
#   Tradition  a short phrase: whose story it is, or who devised the figure and roughly when.
#   Story      a few sentences of original prose. Where a myth has several versions, the best known is
#              told and the others are named; where there is no myth, the entry says so.
#
# Provenance: written for Skyglass from general knowledge of the classical sources (the star-myth
# summaries attributed to Eratosthenes, Hyginus' Astronomica, Ovid, Apollodorus, Ptolemy's Almagest)
# and of the histories of the later constellations (Plancius, Keyser and de Houtman, Hevelius,
# Lacaille). No text was copied from any book or website. It was not checked against those primary
# sources line by line; myths differ between ancient authors, so treat each as "one standard telling".
# The '|' character is the field separator and must not appear in a field.
And|the Chained Princess|ancient|Greek|Andromeda was the daughter of King Cepheus and Queen Cassiopeia of Aethiopia. When Cassiopeia boasted that she was lovelier than the sea nymphs, Poseidon sent the sea monster Cetus to ravage the coast, and an oracle demanded that Andromeda be chained to a rock as an offering. Perseus, flying home after killing Medusa, slew the monster and took her as his wife. Athena placed her among the stars, close to her husband and her parents.
Ant|the Air Pump|modern|Lacaille, 1750s|There is no myth here. Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille named this faint patch after the air pump, the vacuum machine that Robert Boyle, Denis Papin and other 17th-century experimenters used to study air. He made it one of fourteen new southern constellations that honour instruments of science and the arts, charted from the Cape of Good Hope in 1751 and 1752.
Aps|the Bird of Paradise|modern|Keyser and de Houtman, 1590s|There is no myth here. The Dutch navigators Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman charted the southern sky on a voyage to the East Indies in the 1590s, and this is one of twelve figures that came out of it. Johann Bayer drew it in his atlas of 1603. Skins of birds of paradise arrived in Europe without feet, which gave rise to the belief that the birds never landed, and Apus means footless.
Aqr|the Water-bearer|ancient|Greek, from Babylonian|Greek writers usually saw Ganymede, the Trojan prince whom Zeus carried off to pour wine for the gods; others saw Deucalion, who survived the great flood. The picture is older than Greece, since Babylonian star lists already show a water-pourer, called the Great One, in this part of the sky. It lies among the watery constellations: Pisces, Cetus, Eridanus and the Sea-goat.
Aql|the Eagle|ancient|Greek|In Greek myth the eagle was the bird of Zeus and the carrier of his thunderbolts. One story has it swooping on the Trojan prince Ganymede to bring him to Olympus as the gods' cupbearer; another makes it the eagle that Zeus sent to torment Prometheus. Whichever tale is told, a bird of prey has been seen here for a very long time, and Babylonian star lists also name an eagle in this region.
Ara|the Altar|ancient|Greek|The Altar is the one on which, according to Greek tradition, the gods swore an oath of alliance before they went to war against the Titans. When Zeus had won, he set the altar in the heavens as a reminder. It lies low in the south, beside the tail of the Scorpion, and from northern countries it barely clears the horizon.
Ari|the Ram|ancient|Greek|This is the ram with the golden fleece. It was sent to save the children Phrixus and Helle from a plot by their stepmother and flew them eastward. Helle fell into the strait that now bears her name, but Phrixus reached Colchis, sacrificed the ram to Zeus and hung its golden fleece in a sacred grove. That fleece was the prize Jason and the Argonauts later sailed to win.
Aur|the Charioteer|ancient|Greek|Greek writers often named the charioteer Erichthonius, a king of Athens who was said to have invented the four-horse chariot. The bright star Capella, whose name means little she-goat, is linked to Amalthea, the goat that suckled the infant Zeus, and the small triangle of faint stars beside it is known as the Kids. The pentagon of stars is easy to find high in the winter sky.
Boo|the Herdsman|ancient|Greek|The Greeks told several stories about the herdsman. One names him Arcas, the son of Callisto, who was set in the sky near his mother the Great Bear, and Arcturus means guardian of the bear. Another calls him Icarius, the Athenian who was given wine by Dionysus, and a third makes him the inventor of the plough, driving his oxen round the pole. All agree that he follows the Bear across the sky.
Cae|the Chisel|modern|Lacaille, 1750s|There is no myth here. Lacaille named this dim group for the engraver's chisel, which he called les burins, one of the tools of the arts he scattered over the southern sky. It fills a gap between Eridanus and Columba and its brightest star is only about fourth magnitude.
Cam|the Giraffe|modern|Plancius, 1612|There is no classical myth here. The Dutch cartographer Petrus Plancius added the giraffe to his globe in 1612, when the animal was still a curiosity in Europe, and Jakob Bartsch printed it in 1624. Some later writers tied it to the camel that carried Rebecca to Isaac in the Book of Genesis. It is a large and very faint constellation near the north celestial pole; its brightest star is only about fourth magnitude.
Cnc|the Crab|ancient|Greek|While Heracles fought the many-headed Hydra of Lerna, Hera sent a giant crab to pinch his foot and distract him. He crushed it, and Hera, who hated Heracles, set the crab among the stars as a reward for its loyalty. Its stars are faint. At its centre is the Beehive Cluster, which the Greeks called Praesepe, the manger, with two faint stars on either side that were taken for donkeys feeding at it.
CVn|the Hunting Dogs|modern|Hevelius, 1680s|There is no myth here. Johannes Hevelius, a Danzig brewer and astronomer, cut these stars out of the area under the Great Bear's tail and drew them as two hounds, which he named Asterion and Chara, held on a leash by the neighbouring Herdsman as they chase the Bear round the pole. The group holds the globular cluster M3 and the Whirlpool Galaxy.
CMa|the Great Dog|ancient|Greek|The Great Dog follows Orion, the hunter, across the sky; some Greek writers made it Laelaps, the hound that never failed to catch its quarry. Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, shines in its jaw, and its name means scorching. The Greeks blamed the star for the heat of high summer, the dog days, and in Egypt the first dawn rising of Sirius, which the Egyptians called Sopdet, came at about the time the Nile began to flood.
CMi|the Lesser Dog|ancient|Greek|The Lesser Dog is the second of Orion's hounds. Some Greek writers instead identified it with Maera, the dog of Icarius, which led his daughter Erigone to her father's grave and died of grief. Its bright star Procyon means before the dog, because from the Mediterranean it rises a little ahead of Sirius.
Cap|the Sea-goat|ancient|Greek, from Babylonian|In the Greek story the god Pan, fleeing the monster Typhon, leapt into the Nile and changed only half of himself into a fish, ending up with the front of a goat and the tail of a fish; Zeus set this odd creature among the stars. The goat-fish is older than Greece: it was a symbol of the Babylonian god Ea at least three thousand years ago.
Car|the Keel|ancient|Greek, divided by Lacaille|Carina is the keel of the great ship Argo Navis, the vessel in which Jason and his fifty Argonauts sailed to Colchis to win the Golden Fleece. Ptolemy listed the whole ship as one constellation, but it was so large that Lacaille cut it into the Keel, the Stern (Puppis) and the Sails (Vela) in the 1750s. Canopus, the second brightest star in the night sky, is named after the helmsman of a Greek fleet in the story of the Trojan War.
Cas|the Seated Queen|ancient|Greek|Cassiopeia was the wife of King Cepheus and the mother of Andromeda. Her boast that she or her daughter outshone the sea nymphs brought Poseidon's monster upon the kingdom. She was set among the stars on her throne. Some tellers add that, because the throne circles the pole, she hangs upside down for part of each night as her punishment. Her five brightest stars form a W or an M.
Cen|the Centaur|ancient|Greek|The Centaur is usually Chiron, the wise and gentle centaur who taught heroes such as Achilles, Jason and Asclepius. One of Heracles' poisoned arrows wounded him by accident, and since he was immortal he could not die of the pain; he gave up his immortality, and Zeus placed him in the sky. A rival tradition names the centaur Pholus. Alpha Centauri is the nearest star system to the Sun.
Cep|the King|ancient|Greek|Cepheus was the king of Aethiopia, husband of Cassiopeia and father of Andromeda. When the sea monster threatened his coast he followed the oracle and gave up his daughter, who was saved at the last moment by Perseus. The whole royal family is placed together in the northern sky, along with Perseus and the monster. His figure is a faint house shape.
Cet|the Sea Monster|ancient|Greek|Cetus is the monster that Poseidon sent against Aethiopia to punish Queen Cassiopeia for her boasting. The Greek word ketos meant any huge sea creature, and the figure is often drawn as a whale today. It was about to devour the chained Andromeda when Perseus destroyed it. Cetus lies in the faint, watery part of the sky; its star Mira is a famous variable that swells and fades over about eleven months.
Cha|the Chameleon|modern|Keyser and de Houtman, 1590s|There is no myth here. This small figure near the south celestial pole is one of the twelve drawn up from the southern observations of the Dutch navigators Keyser and de Houtman in the 1590s, and it appeared in Bayer's atlas of 1603. Its name comes from the Greek for ground lion.
Cir|the Compasses|modern|Lacaille, 1750s|There is no myth here. Lacaille named this tiny constellation after the drafting compasses, the dividers used by draughtsmen and navigators. It is squeezed between Centaurus and the Southern Triangle, right next to the bright pair of Alpha and Beta Centauri, and is one of his fourteen constellations of instruments.
Col|the Dove|modern|Plancius, 1592|The dove was cut from the stars south of the Great Dog by Plancius in 1592. He, a minister as well as a cartographer, meant it as the dove that Noah sent out from the ark, which came back with an olive leaf. Other writers have connected it with the dove that guided the Argonauts through the Clashing Rocks, which fits its place beside the old ship Argo.
Com|Berenice's Hair|modern|Conon of Samos, 3rd century BC; named in the 1530s|Queen Berenice II of Egypt cut off her hair and offered it at a temple, to ask for her husband Ptolemy III to come home safely from war. When the lock disappeared, the court astronomer Conon of Samos declared that the gods had taken it to the sky. The poet Callimachus wrote about it, and Catullus later translated his poem. Ptolemy still counted the stars as part of the Lion's tail; Caspar Vopel gave them their own constellation on a globe in the 1530s and Tycho Brahe kept it.
CrA|the Southern Crown|ancient|Greek|No firm myth belongs to this small arc of stars south of the Archer. Ptolemy listed it as a wreath, and later retellings tie it variously to the Archer or to Dionysus, but none is well established. Its stars make a neat arc, close to the Milky Way next to the Archer.
CrB|the Northern Crown|ancient|Greek|The Northern Crown is the jewelled wreath that the god Dionysus gave Ariadne, the Cretan princess who helped Theseus escape from the labyrinth and was then abandoned by him on the island of Naxos. Dionysus found her, married her and set the crown in the sky. Its brightest star is Alphecca, also called Gemma, the jewel.
Crv|the Raven|ancient|Greek|Apollo sent his raven with a cup to fetch water from a spring. The bird lingered beside a fig tree, waiting for the figs to ripen, and then flew back with a water snake in its claws, blaming the snake for the delay. Apollo saw through the lie and flung the raven, the cup and the snake into the sky; the raven perches on the snake, which stretches out far across the heavens, and can never reach the cup to drink.
Crt|the Cup|ancient|Greek|The Cup is the one that Apollo gave his raven to fetch water, in the story told under the Raven. The raven's excuse was a snake, Hydra, which lies across all three constellations, and Apollo set them in the sky so that the thirsty bird would never be able to drink from the cup. Other tellers give it other owners, such as Dionysus.
Cru|the Southern Cross|modern|Plancius and Hondius, about 1600|The smallest of the 88 constellations is also the most famous one in the south. The ancient Greeks, who could see it low on their southern horizon, counted its stars as part of the Centaur's legs, but the slow wobble of the Earth's axis has carried it far to the south since then. European navigators wrote about it in the early 1500s, and Petrus Plancius and Jodocus Hondius gave it its own constellation around 1600. It appears on the flags of Australia, New Zealand, Papua New Guinea and Samoa.
Cyg|the Swan|ancient|Greek|Several swans compete for this figure. In one story Zeus took the form of a swan to visit Leda, who bore Helen and the twins Castor and Pollux. In another, Cycnus, a friend of Phaethon, dived again and again into the river for his body after Phaethon's fall and was turned into a swan by the gods. A third tradition makes it Orpheus, changed into a swan after his death and placed beside his lyre. Its bright stars form the Northern Cross.
Del|the Dolphin|ancient|Greek|When the sea god Poseidon fell in love with the nymph Amphitrite, she fled from him. A dolphin searched until it found her and persuaded her to return, and Poseidon rewarded it with a place in the stars. Another tale says it is the dolphin that carried the poet Arion to safety after sailors threw him overboard. The small kite of stars is easy to recognise.
Dor|the Dolphinfish|modern|Keyser and de Houtman, 1590s|There is no myth here. Dorado is the Spanish and Portuguese name of the dolphinfish or mahi-mahi, a fish that Dutch sailors on the voyage to the East Indies saw leaping in the tropical sea; the figure is among the twelve that came from their southern observations in the 1590s. It contains most of the Large Magellanic Cloud, a satellite galaxy of ours about 160,000 light years away.
Dra|the Dragon|ancient|Greek|The Dragon is often said to be Ladon, the serpent that guarded the golden apples of the Hesperides until Heracles killed it. In another story it was a dragon thrown by Athena into the sky during the war between the gods and the Giants, so violently that it froze coiled round the pole. Around 2800 BC the pole star was Thuban, a star in Draco.
Equ|the Foal|ancient|Greek|The Foal is a tiny, faint constellation, and the ancient accounts of it are thin and do not agree. One story names it Celeris, the brother of Pegasus; another says it is the horse that Hermes gave to Castor. Ptolemy listed its stars, and it lies just before Pegasus, the winged horse, as he rises.
Eri|the River|ancient|Greek|The Greeks most often connected the River with Eridanus, the river into which Phaethon fell when Zeus struck him with a thunderbolt after he lost control of his father Helios's sun chariot. His sisters wept by the bank and were turned into poplars whose tears were amber. Other writers identified the sky river with the Nile. The long winding figure ends at Achernar, whose name means the end of the river.
For|the Furnace|modern|Lacaille, 1750s|There is no myth here. Lacaille named it the chemical furnace, after the apparatus of the new science of chemistry in his day, one of his fourteen figures of instruments and the arts. It is a faint group in the long curve of Eridanus, and it contains a rich cluster of distant galaxies.
Gem|the Twins|ancient|Greek|The twins are Castor and Pollux, the sons of Leda. Pollux was a son of Zeus and immortal; Castor was the son of the mortal Tyndareus. When Castor was killed in a fight, Pollux begged Zeus to let him share his immortality, and the two brothers were placed in the sky together. They were the patron gods of sailors, and a sailor's St. Elmo's fire was once called their sign.
Gru|the Crane|modern|Keyser and de Houtman, 1590s|There is no myth here. The Crane is another of the twelve figures taken from the observations of the Dutch navigators Keyser and de Houtman in the 1590s and first drawn in Bayer's atlas of 1603. The long, graceful bird lies south of the Southern Fish, with its neck stretched toward the south.
Her|the Hero Heracles|ancient|Greek|The Greeks first knew this figure as the Kneeler, a man on one knee whose name nobody remembered. Later writers identified him with Heracles, the hero of the twelve labours. This explains his neighbours in the sky: the Lion, the Hydra and the Dragon of the Hesperides are all creatures he fought. The Great Globular Cluster, M13, is near the corner of the central Keystone of stars.
Hor|the Pendulum Clock|modern|Lacaille, 1750s|There is no myth here. Lacaille named this faint group for the pendulum clock invented by Christiaan Huygens in the 1650s,, a great advance in accurate timekeeping. He made it one of his fourteen figures of scientific instruments and the arts. It meanders across a dim part of the southern sky east of Achernar.
Hya|the Water Snake|ancient|Greek|This is the Lernaean Hydra, the many-headed water serpent that Heracles killed in the second of his labours. Each time he cut off a head, two more grew in its place, until his nephew Iolaus burned the stumps. It is the largest of the 88 constellations, stretching more than a quarter of the way round the sky, with the Crab, the Raven and the Cup riding along it.
Hyi|the Lesser Water Snake|modern|Keyser and de Houtman, 1590s|There is no myth here. This southern serpent is one of the twelve figures drawn up from the observations of the Dutch navigators Keyser and de Houtman in the 1590s. Its name is the male counterpart of the ancient Hydra, which can lead to confusion: the two lie far apart in the sky.
Ind|the Indian|modern|Keyser and de Houtman, 1590s|There is no myth here. One of the twelve figures from the Dutch navigators' southern observations in the 1590s, it is drawn as a native person holding arrows. Which people the navigators had in mind, whether from the East Indies they visited or elsewhere, is not recorded and is uncertain.
Lac|the Lizard|modern|Hevelius, 1680s|There is no myth here. Hevelius added the lizard in the 1680s in the space between Cygnus and Andromeda. Its stars form a zigzag W shape, rather like that of Cassiopeia, but dimmer.
Leo|the Lion|ancient|Greek|The Lion is usually the Nemean lion, a beast with a hide no weapon could pierce. Heracles, in the first of his labours, strangled it with his bare hands and skinned it with its own claws, and wore its pelt ever after. Zeus placed the lion in the heavens. The Sickle of stars that outlines its head and chest ends with Regulus, the little king.
LMi|the Lesser Lion|modern|Hevelius, 1680s|There is no myth here. Hevelius invented the Lesser Lion in the 1680s to fill a dark gap between the Lion and the Great Bear. It is a faint constellation with no bright stars and no story of its own.
Lep|the Hare|ancient|Greek|The Greeks told no particular tale of the Hare. It is simply the quarry of Orion the hunter, running just below his feet, while the Great Dog bounds after it. Because Lepus lies so close beneath Orion, the sky itself seems to carry the hunt.
Lib|the Scales|ancient|Roman, from Babylonian|The Greeks saw these stars as the Claws of the Scorpion. The Romans, around the time of Julius Caesar, saw a pair of scales instead, a symbol of justice linked with Astraea, the Maiden next door in Virgo. A set of scales also appears in Babylonian star lists, so the idea may be much older. Libra is the only sign of the zodiac that is not a living creature.
Lup|the Wolf|ancient|Greek|The Greeks knew these stars only as the Beast, a wild animal which the Centaur is holding up on his spear. The Romans translated it as a wolf. Some later writers linked it to Lycaon, the king who served human flesh to Zeus and was turned into a wolf as punishment, but that is a late connection.
Lyn|the Lynx|modern|Hevelius, 1680s|There is no myth here. Hevelius filled the dim stretch between the Great Bear and the Charioteer with a lynx in the 1680s and joked that one would need the eyes of a lynx to see it. It is a long, faint line of stars, and its brightest star is only about third magnitude.
Lyr|the Lyre|ancient|Greek|Hermes made the first lyre from a tortoise shell and gave it to Apollo, who passed it to his son Orpheus. Orpheus played so beautifully that animals, trees and even stones followed him, and he charmed the rulers of the dead. When he was killed by a band of women, his lyre was set in the sky. The bright blue-white star Vega marks the lyre.
Men|Table Mountain|modern|Lacaille, 1750s|There is no myth here. Lacaille named this constellation for Table Mountain above Cape Town, where he set up his observatory in 1751. It is the only constellation named for a feature of the Earth. It is also one of the faintest, with a brightest star of only fifth magnitude, and it holds part of the Large Magellanic Cloud.
Mic|the Microscope|modern|Lacaille, 1750s|There is no myth here. Lacaille honoured the compound microscope, one of the instruments of the new science of his day. Its stars are faint, and it sits in a dark patch of the southern sky between the Archer and the Southern Fish.
Mon|the Unicorn|modern|Plancius, about 1612|There is no classical myth here. Petrus Plancius first drew the unicorn, an animal of medieval legend, about 1612, and Jakob Bartsch printed it in 1624. The faint figure lies on the winter Milky Way between Orion and the Great Dog, and holds the Rosette Nebula and the Christmas Tree Cluster.
Mus|the Fly|modern|Keyser and de Houtman, 1590s|There is no myth here. One of the twelve southern figures from the 1590s, it appears as the Bee in Bayer's atlas of 1603 and as the Fly in later maps. It lies just south of the Southern Cross, in a rich part of the Milky Way.
Nor|the Carpenter's Square|modern|Lacaille, 1750s|There is no myth here. Lacaille called it the square and the rule, the surveyor's and carpenter's tools; the Latin name Norma means the square. It is one of his fourteen figures honouring the instruments of science and the arts, in a crowded part of the Milky Way beside the Scorpion's tail.
Oct|the Octant|modern|Lacaille, 1750s|There is no myth here. Lacaille named it for the octant, the reflecting navigation instrument that John Hadley developed in the 1730s, which was a forerunner of the sextant. The south celestial pole lies inside Octans, but the nearest star to it, Sigma Octantis, is faint, so the southern sky has no bright pole star.
Oph|the Serpent-bearer|ancient|Greek|The figure is Asclepius, the physician-god who learned the art of healing, and some said of raising the dead, from a serpent. Zeus struck him down with a thunderbolt for breaking the order between mortals and the dead, but set him in the sky at Apollo's request. The serpent in his hands is the constellation Serpens, which is divided into a head and a tail on either side of him.
Ori|the Hunter|ancient|Greek|The best known story says that Orion, a giant hunter, boasted that he would kill every animal on Earth. Gaia, the Earth, sent a scorpion that stung him dead. Zeus placed both in the sky on opposite sides, so Orion sets as the Scorpion rises. In another version Artemis shot him by mistake after being tricked by her brother Apollo. The three stars of his belt and the bright Betelgeuse and Rigel make him easy to find.
Pav|the Peacock|modern|Keyser and de Houtman, 1590s|There is no myth here. In Greek myth the peacock was Hera's bird, carrying the eyes of the giant Argus on its tail, but that link was not part of the original figure. The Dutch navigators named it for the bird they saw in the East Indies in the 1590s, and Bayer drew it in 1603.
Peg|the Winged Horse|ancient|Greek|Pegasus sprang from the blood of Medusa when Perseus cut off her head. The hero Bellerophon rode him to kill the Chimera. When Bellerophon tried to fly to Olympus on him, Pegasus carried on without his rider and was given the task of carrying Zeus's thunderbolts. A strike of his hoof on Mount Helicon made the spring that inspired poets. Only the front half of the horse is drawn, and four of his stars form the Great Square.
Per|the Hero Perseus|ancient|Greek|Perseus was the son of Zeus and Danae. He was sent to bring back the head of Medusa, the Gorgon whose gaze turned people to stone, and he did it with the help of winged sandals from Hermes, a polished shield from Athena and a curved sword. On the way home he found the chained Andromeda and killed the sea monster to free her. The star Algol marks Medusa's head, and it fades and brightens about every three days.
Phe|the Phoenix|modern|Keyser and de Houtman, 1590s|There is no myth of the constellation itself. The phoenix is a legendary bird that burns and is reborn from its own ashes, a story told by Greek and Egyptian writers, but this figure is one of the twelve from the Dutch navigators' southern observations of the 1590s, and its creators attached no particular story to it.
Pic|the Painter's Easel|modern|Lacaille, 1750s|There is no myth here. Lacaille named it the painter's easel, one of the arts he honoured. It is a faint figure next to the brilliant star Canopus, and holds Kapteyn's Star, a nearby red dwarf that moves unusually fast across the sky.
PsA|the Southern Fish|ancient|Greek|The Southern Fish lies with its mouth open under the stream of water that the Water-bearer pours, drinking it. Greek writers called it the parent of the two fishes in Pisces. Fomalhaut, its bright star, takes its name from an Arabic phrase that means the mouth of the fish.
Psc|the Fishes|ancient|Greek|When the monster Typhon attacked the gods, Aphrodite and her son Eros escaped by throwing themselves into a river and changing into fishes. They tied themselves together with a cord so as not to be separated, and the two fishes with their cord became the constellation. It is a large but faint figure under Pegasus and Andromeda, and the Sun is in it at the spring equinox.
Pup|the Stern|ancient|Greek, divided by Lacaille|Puppis is the stern, or poop deck, of Argo Navis, the ship in which Jason and the Argonauts sailed for the Golden Fleece. Ptolemy listed the whole vessel as one huge constellation; Lacaille divided it into the Keel (Carina), the Stern and the Sails (Vela) in the 1750s. It lies in a rich stretch of the winter Milky Way, full of faint stars and open clusters.
Pyx|the Mariner's Compass|modern|Lacaille, 1750s|There is no myth here. Lacaille named it for the mariner's compass. It sits within the area of the old ship Argo, near its stern and sails, where some earlier charts had drawn the ship's mast, so it fits there as one of its navigation tools. It is a small, faint figure.
Ret|the Reticle|modern|Lacaille, 1750s|There is no myth here. Lacaille named it after the reticle, the small grid of fine threads in his telescope's eyepiece that he used to measure the positions of stars. It is a small diamond of stars near the Large Magellanic Cloud.
Sge|the Arrow|ancient|Greek|The Greeks did not agree about which arrow this is. It might be the one that Heracles shot at the eagle that tormented Prometheus; it might be Apollo's arrow, with which he avenged his son by killing the Cyclopes; or it might be one of Eros's. It is a tiny but very clear arrow shape, and the third smallest of the 88 constellations.
Sgr|the Archer|ancient|Greek, from Babylonian|Greek writers disagreed about the archer. Some thought it was the centaur Chiron; others thought it was Crotus, a satyr who lived among the Muses on Mount Helicon and invented archery. The picture of a bow-wielding centaur comes from Mesopotamia, and the Archer lies in the direction of the centre of our galaxy. Its brightest stars form the Teapot.
Sco|the Scorpion|ancient|Greek, from Babylonian|The Scorpion is the creature that killed Orion, sent by Gaia or by Artemis or Apollo, depending on the teller; it still chases him across the sky, and as it rises he sets. The brilliant red star Antares in its heart means rival of Mars. A scorpion was drawn in this part of the sky by the Babylonians as well.
Scl|the Sculptor's Studio|modern|Lacaille, 1750s|There is no myth here. Lacaille called it the sculptor's workshop, one of his fourteen figures honouring the arts and sciences. It is a faint group, but it contains the south galactic pole, which means that it looks out of the Milky Way's disc into deep intergalactic space, so many distant galaxies show up here.
Sct|the Shield|modern|Hevelius, 1684|The Shield was devised by Hevelius in 1684 and named Scutum Sobiescianum, Sobieski's Shield, in honour of King John III Sobieski of Poland, who had led the relief of Vienna from the Ottoman siege the year before. It is a small group of faint stars set in a dense, bright cloud of the Milky Way, and it holds the Wild Duck Cluster, M11.
Ser|the Serpent|ancient|Greek|The Serpent is the one Ophiuchus holds, and it is the only constellation split into two parts: the head, Caput, on one side of him, and the tail, Cauda, on the other. The serpent is linked to the physician-god Asclepius, who in myth learned the art of healing from a serpent, and the snake has been a sign of medicine ever since.
Sex|the Sextant|modern|Hevelius, 1680s|There is no myth here. Hevelius named it after the large brass sextant he used to measure star positions with great accuracy in his observatory in Danzig. A fire in 1679 destroyed his instruments and his observatory; the constellation recalls the sextant. It is a dim figure on the celestial equator.
Tau|the Bull|ancient|Greek|In the best known story Zeus took the shape of a gentle white bull to carry the Phoenician princess Europa across the sea to Crete, where she became the mother of King Minos. Only the front half of the bull is drawn, as though it were charging out of the sea. In its shoulder are the Pleiades, and its face is the V of the Hyades, with the red eye of Aldebaran.
Tel|the Telescope|modern|Lacaille, 1750s|There is no myth here. Lacaille named it for the telescope itself, which has been the main tool of astronomy since Galileo. It is a faint group below the Archer and the Southern Crown.
Tri|the Triangle|ancient|Greek|The Triangle has no real myth. It was named for its shape, which looks like the Greek letter delta, and writers linked it with the delta of the Nile or, in a Roman story, with the triangular island of Sicily, which was dear to the goddess Ceres. It is a small group next to Andromeda and Aries.
TrA|the Southern Triangle|modern|Keyser and de Houtman, 1590s|There is no myth here. It is one of the twelve figures from the southern observations of the Dutch navigators in the 1590s, and appeared in Bayer's atlas in 1603. Its three brightest stars form a clear triangle, and the brightest, Atria, is an orange giant.
Tuc|the Toucan|modern|Keyser and de Houtman, 1590s|There is no myth here. The toucan is a bird of South America that the navigators and Bayer knew from reports from Brazil, and it is one of the twelve figures from the 1590s. It holds the Small Magellanic Cloud and the great globular cluster 47 Tucanae, the second brightest in the sky.
UMa|the Great Bear|ancient|Greek|Callisto was a nymph who followed the goddess Artemis. Zeus fell in love with her, and either Hera or Artemis changed her into a bear; years later her son Arcas, out hunting, nearly killed her before Zeus placed them both in the sky as the Great Bear and the Herdsman. Hera, still angry, saw to it that the bears could never dip into the ocean to bathe. The seven bright stars form the Plough or the Big Dipper.
UMi|the Little Bear|ancient|Greek|The Little Bear is a smaller companion to the Great Bear, and the Greeks gave it different identities: Arcas, or one of the nymphs who nursed the infant Zeus. It is said that Thales introduced it as a guide for navigators, the Phoenician Bear. At the end of its tail is Polaris, the North Star, which today sits almost exactly at the north celestial pole.
Vel|the Sails|ancient|Greek, divided by Lacaille|Vela is the sail of Argo Navis, the ship of Jason and the Argonauts. Ptolemy listed the whole vessel as one constellation; Lacaille cut it into the Keel (Carina), the Stern (Puppis) and the Sails in the 1750s. Bayer lettered the stars of the whole ship in one sequence, so Alpha and Beta fell in the Keel, and Vela itself has no Alpha or Beta.
Vir|the Maiden|ancient|Greek|The Maiden is most often Astraea, the goddess of justice, the last of the immortals to leave the Earth when the Golden Age declined. The bright star Spica, whose name means ear of grain, links her to Demeter, goddess of harvests, or to her daughter Persephone, who spends the spring and summer above ground. It is the second largest constellation in the sky.
Vol|the Flying Fish|modern|Keyser and de Houtman, 1590s|There is no myth here. The flying fish, which skim over the tropical sea and which the Dutch navigators saw on their voyage to the East Indies, is another of their twelve southern figures from the 1590s. It lies near the Large Magellanic Cloud and the keel of the old ship Argo.
Vul|the Fox|modern|Hevelius, 1680s|There is no myth here. Hevelius drew a fox with a goose in its jaws in the 1680s, which he named Vulpecula cum Ansere; the goose was later dropped. It lies on the Milky Way and contains the Dumbbell Nebula, the Coathanger, and the pulsar that Jocelyn Bell found in 1967.
