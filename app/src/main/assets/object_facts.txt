# A little more about the things on the sky's details cards: Key|Name|About|Fact
#
#   Key    what it describes: @SUN, @MOON or a planet (a SkyBody name); "HR n" for a star (its Bright Star
#          Catalogue number); otherwise a deep-sky object's id, the first designation in deep_sky.txt ("M31").
#   Name   the name the card shows (the star's proper name, the object's title, the body's English name), so
#          a mistyped key is caught by the tests rather than giving one object another's words.
#   About  one or two sentences: what it is, and roughly how far or how big.
#   Fact   one or two sentences of "did you know?".
#
# Keep both short: this is a card on a sky view, not an encyclopaedia (the tests cap each field's length).
# Every Sun, Moon, planet and deep-sky object has an entry; stars only where there is something to say.
#
# Provenance: written for Skyglass from general astronomical knowledge; no text was copied from a book or
# website. It was not checked line by line against primary sources. Distances are rounded and are the
# commonly quoted modern values, which differ between sources by 10 % or more (more for supernova
# remnants, planetary nebulae and the most distant galaxies). Star entries avoid distances, because the
# card's distance row comes from the 1991 catalogue's parallax and would disagree with a modern figure.
# The '|' character is the field separator and must not appear in a field.

# --- Sun, Moon and planets ---
@SUN|Sun|Our star: a ball of hydrogen and helium 109 times as wide as the Earth, 150 million km away. Never look at it through binoculars or a telescope without a proper solar filter.|More than a million Earths would fit inside the Sun, and it holds about 99.8 % of all the mass in the Solar System. Its light takes about 8 minutes 20 seconds to reach us.
@MOON|Moon|The Earth's only natural satellite, about a quarter of the Earth's width and 384,400 km away on average. It turns once for each orbit, so it always shows us the same face.|The Moon is drifting away from the Earth by about 3.8 cm a year, roughly as fast as fingernails grow. Twelve people walked on it between 1969 and 1972.
@MERCURY|Mercury|The smallest planet and the closest to the Sun, which it circles every 88 days. It never strays far from the Sun in our sky, so look for it low in the twilight.|A day on Mercury, from one sunrise to the next, lasts 176 Earth days, two of its years. Its surface swings from about 430 °C by day to −180 °C at night.
@VENUS|Venus|Nearly the Earth's twin in size, wrapped in thick clouds of sulphuric acid. After the Moon it is the brightest thing in the night sky: the morning or evening star.|Venus is the hottest planet, about 465 °C at the surface, because its thick carbon dioxide air traps the heat. It spins backwards, so slowly that its day is longer than its year.
@MARS|Mars|The red planet, about half the Earth's width. Iron oxide, rust, in its dust gives it its colour, and it has two small moons, Phobos and Deimos.|Mars has the tallest volcano in the Solar System, Olympus Mons, about 22 km high, two and a half times the height of Everest. A day on Mars is only about 40 minutes longer than ours.
@JUPITER|Jupiter|The largest planet, a gas giant 11 times the Earth's width with dozens of moons. Even small binoculars show its four biggest moons as points of light beside it.|Jupiter's Great Red Spot is a storm wider than the Earth that has been raging for well over 150 years. The planet spins in under 10 hours, the shortest day of any planet.
@SATURN|Saturn|The ringed gas giant, nine times the Earth's width. The rings are countless pieces of ice and rock, and even a small telescope shows them.|Saturn is so light for its size that it would float in water, given a big enough bath. Its rings are about 270,000 km across but in places only about 10 m thick.
@URANUS|Uranus|An ice giant four times the Earth's width, coloured blue-green by methane. It is at the very limit of naked-eye visibility from a dark site; binoculars show it as a star-like dot.|Uranus spins tipped on its side, so each pole gets about 42 years of daylight and then 42 years of night. It was the first planet found with a telescope, by William Herschel in 1781.
@NEPTUNE|Neptune|The farthest planet, an ice giant about four times the Earth's width and 30 times as far from the Sun as we are. It is too faint to see without binoculars or a telescope.|Neptune was found by mathematics: in 1846 Urbain Le Verrier worked out where it must be from its pull on Uranus, and it was seen within a degree of his prediction. Its winds blow at over 2,000 km/h.

# --- Stars (by HR number) ---
HR 2491|Sirius|The brightest star in the night sky, a hot white star about twice the Sun's mass and one of our nearest neighbours. Its glare hides a faint companion, the white dwarf Sirius B.|Sirius B is about the size of the Earth but nearly as massive as the Sun: a sugar cube of it would weigh about as much as a car. Low in the sky, Sirius twinkles in flashes of every colour.
HR 2326|Canopus|The second-brightest star in the night sky, a white giant in the southern constellation Carina. It never rises from the British Isles, most of Europe or Canada.|Spacecraft have used Canopus as a guide star to keep themselves pointed the right way, because it is bright and lies far from the Sun's path across the sky.
HR 5340|Arcturus|The brightest star in the northern half of the sky, an orange giant about 25 times the Sun's width. Follow the curve of the Plough's handle and "arc to Arcturus".|Arcturus switched on the lights of the 1933 Chicago World's Fair: its light, focused onto a photocell, was thought to have left the star at the time of the previous Chicago fair, in 1893.
HR 5459|Rigil Kentaurus|Alpha Centauri, the nearest star system to the Sun. To the eye it is one star, but a small telescope splits it into two Sun-like stars that circle each other every 80 years.|A third, faint red dwarf of the system, Proxima Centauri, is the closest known star to us, 4.2 light years away, and has a planet about as massive as the Earth.
HR 7001|Vega|A bright blue-white star, one corner of the Summer Triangle with Deneb and Altair. It spins so fast that it bulges at its equator.|Vega was the first star after the Sun to be photographed, in 1850. In about 12,000 years the slow wobble of the Earth's axis will make it the pole star.
HR 1708|Capella|A yellow star that is really two giant stars orbiting each other every 104 days, too close to separate in any ordinary telescope; two faint red dwarfs further out make it a family of four.|Capella means "little she-goat" in Latin. North of about 44° N it never sets, circling the pole all night.
HR 1713|Rigel|Orion's bright foot, a blue-white supergiant tens of thousands of times as luminous as the Sun.|Although labelled beta, Rigel is usually brighter than Betelgeuse, Orion's alpha. Its name comes from the Arabic for "the foot".
HR 2943|Procyon|The brightest star in Canis Minor and one of the Sun's nearer neighbours, a yellow-white star a little bigger and brighter than the Sun.|Its name means "before the dog" in Greek, because from northern lands it rises shortly before Sirius, the Dog Star. Like Sirius, it has a white dwarf companion.
HR 472|Achernar|The brightest star of Eridanus, at the river's southern end: a hot blue star never seen from north of about 33° N.|Achernar spins so fast that it is one of the flattest stars known, at least a third wider through its equator than through its poles.
HR 2061|Betelgeuse|Orion's shoulder, a red supergiant so large that, in place of the Sun, it would reach out past the orbit of Mars. Its brightness changes as it pulsates.|Betelgeuse will end as a supernova some time in the next 100,000 years or so, and will then shine about as brightly as a half Moon. In 2019-20 it dimmed sharply behind a cloud of dust it had thrown off.
HR 5267|Hadar|Beta Centauri, a hot blue giant star. With Rigil Kentaurus it forms the two Pointers that lead to the Southern Cross.|The Pointers look like a pair, but Hadar is nearly 90 times farther away than Alpha Centauri.
HR 7557|Altair|A white star, one corner of the Summer Triangle, and one of the closer bright stars.|Altair spins in well under half a day, so fast that it is visibly flattened. In 2007 it became the first ordinary star, other than the Sun, to have its surface imaged.
HR 1457|Aldebaran|The red eye of Taurus the Bull, an orange giant about 44 times the Sun's width. It looks like part of the V-shaped Hyades cluster but lies less than half as far away.|The Pioneer 10 space probe, launched in 1972, is drifting in the direction of Aldebaran and would get there in about two million years.
HR 6134|Antares|The red heart of the Scorpion, a red supergiant hundreds of times the Sun's width, with a hot blue companion that shows in a telescope when the air is steady.|Its name means "rival of Mars" (anti-Ares), because its colour matches the red planet, which often passes near it.
HR 5056|Spica|The brightest star in Virgo: two hot blue stars so close together that they orbit each other in four days and are pulled into egg shapes.|Comparing Spica's position with older records, Hipparchus discovered the slow wobble of the Earth's axis around 130 BC.
HR 2990|Pollux|The brighter of the Gemini twins, an orange giant and one of the nearest giant stars to the Sun.|Pollux has a planet, a gas giant at least twice as massive as Jupiter, found in 2006. Its twin Castor is labelled alpha but is the fainter of the two.
HR 8728|Fomalhaut|A young white star, the only bright star in a large empty stretch of the autumn sky. Its name means "the mouth of the fish" in Arabic.|Fomalhaut is circled by a wide ring of dusty debris, like a much bigger version of our Kuiper Belt, which the Hubble and James Webb telescopes have photographed.
HR 4853|Mimosa|Beta Crucis, the second-brightest star of the Southern Cross, a hot blue giant.|The Southern Cross, Mimosa included, appears on the flags of Australia, New Zealand, Brazil, Samoa and Papua New Guinea.
HR 7924|Deneb|The tail of Cygnus the Swan and a corner of the Summer Triangle, a white supergiant. Its distance is poorly known, perhaps 1,500 to 2,600 light years, one of the most distant stars easily seen.|Deneb gives out tens of thousands of times as much light as the Sun. If it were as close as Vega, it would outshine every planet and cast shadows at night.
HR 4730|Acrux|Alpha Crucis, the foot of the Southern Cross and the most southerly first-magnitude star. A small telescope splits it into two blue-white stars.|The Southern Cross is the smallest of the 88 constellations, yet it is one of the best known.
HR 3982|Regulus|The heart of Leo the Lion, a blue-white star that sits almost on the ecliptic, so the Moon often passes in front of it.|Regulus spins once in about 16 hours, close to the speed at which it would fly apart, so it is flattened into an egg shape.
HR 2618|Adhara|Epsilon Canis Majoris, a blue giant in the Great Dog.|About 4.7 million years ago Adhara passed much closer to the Sun and was the brightest star in the sky, as bright as Venus.
HR 4763|Gacrux|Gamma Crucis, the top of the Southern Cross, a red giant whose orange colour stands out against its blue-white neighbours.|Gacrux is the nearest red giant of its kind (spectral class M) to the Sun.
HR 6527|Shaula|The sting in the Scorpion's tail, a hot blue star that is really a group of three or more stars.|Shaula means "the raised tail" in Arabic. With its close neighbour Lesath it forms a pair that some call the Cat's Eyes.
HR 1790|Bellatrix|Orion's other shoulder, a hot blue-white giant, much nearer and less luminous than Betelgeuse.|Bellatrix means "female warrior" in Latin, and it is sometimes called the Amazon Star.
HR 1791|Elnath|The tip of one of the Bull's horns, a blue-white star once shared with Auriga, where it also made a corner of the Charioteer's pentagon.|Elnath lies close to the point in the sky directly opposite the centre of the Milky Way, so looking at it you are looking out through our galaxy's rim.
HR 3685|Miaplacidus|Beta Carinae, a white star in the far southern sky.|Its name mixes Arabic and Latin and may mean "placid waters". From anywhere south of about 20° S it never sets.
HR 1903|Alnilam|The middle star of Orion's Belt, a blue supergiant hundreds of thousands of times as luminous as the Sun.|Its name is Arabic for "the string of pearls", the old name of the whole Belt. Follow the Belt down to Sirius, or up to Aldebaran.
HR 8425|Alnair|Alpha Gruis, the brightest star of the Crane, a blue-white star in the southern sky.|Alnair means "the bright one", from an Arabic name for the bright star of the fish's tail, from before these stars became the Crane.
HR 4905|Alioth|The brightest star of the Great Bear, the first star of the Plough's handle, next to the bowl.|Five of the Plough's seven stars, Alioth among them, travel through space together as siblings born in the same cloud. Dubhe and Alkaid, at the two ends, are not part of the family.
HR 3207|Regor|Gamma Velorum, a pairing of a blue supergiant and a Wolf-Rayet star, one of the hottest and most massive kinds of star.|The name began as a joke: Apollo astronaut Gus Grissom used it as a navigation star and named it after fellow astronaut Roger Chaffee, spelled backwards.
HR 1017|Mirfak|Alpha Persei, a yellow-white supergiant, the brightest star of Perseus.|Mirfak sits among a loose scatter of bright blue stars, the Alpha Persei Cluster, which is lovely in binoculars.
HR 4301|Dubhe|The front of the Plough's bowl. With Merak it makes the Pointers: follow the line from Merak through Dubhe about five times as far to reach Polaris.|Dubhe is an orange giant and does not belong to the family of stars that make up most of the Plough, so over tens of thousands of years the shape will change.
HR 6879|Kaus Australis|Epsilon Sagittarii, the brightest star of the Archer and a corner of the Teapot.|Its name means "southern part of the bow", half Arabic and half Latin. The "steam" rising from the Teapot's spout is the Milky Way, towards the centre of our galaxy.
HR 3307|Avior|Epsilon Carinae, an orange giant paired with a hot blue star. It is a corner of the False Cross, which is often mistaken for the Southern Cross.|The name Avior is not old: it was given to the star in the 1930s for a navigation almanac used by the Royal Air Force.
HR 5191|Alkaid|The end of the Plough's handle, the Great Bear's tail, a hot blue star.|Its name comes from the Arabic for "the leader of the mourners", from a tradition that saw the bowl as a coffin followed by mourners.
HR 2088|Menkalinan|Beta Aurigae, a pair of white stars that orbit each other in under four days.|The two stars eclipse each other, but each eclipse dims it by less than a tenth of a magnitude, too little to notice by eye.
HR 6217|Atria|Alpha Trianguli Australis, an orange giant at a corner of the Southern Triangle.|Atria is simply a contraction of its Latin designation, Alpha Trianguli Australis.
HR 7790|Peacock|Alpha Pavonis, a blue-white star, the brightest in the Peacock.|Like Avior, it was named in the 1930s for an air navigation almanac; before that it had no common name.
HR 2891|Castor|The fainter of the Gemini twins, though labelled alpha. A telescope splits it into two white stars.|Castor is a family of six stars: three pairs, all bound together by gravity, that look like one point of light.
HR 3748|Alphard|Alpha Hydrae, an orange giant and the only bright star in a large dim stretch of sky.|Its name means "the solitary one" in Arabic, because there are no other bright stars near it.
HR 617|Hamal|Alpha Arietis, an orange giant at the head of the Ram.|About 2,000 years ago the Sun was in Aries at the spring equinox, which is why that point is still called the First Point of Aries, although the Earth's wobble has since moved it into Pisces.
HR 424|Polaris|The North Star, less than a degree from the north celestial pole, so it barely moves while the sky turns around it. Its height above the horizon is roughly your latitude.|Polaris is only about the 50th brightest star. It has not always been the pole star: nearly 5,000 years ago it was Thuban, in Draco.
HR 188|Diphda|Beta Ceti, an orange giant, the brightest star in Cetus the Sea Monster.|Though labelled beta, it is usually brighter than Menkar, Cetus' alpha. Diphda means "the frog" in Arabic.
HR 1948|Alnitak|The eastern star of Orion's Belt, a hot blue supergiant that is really three stars.|Just below it lies the Horsehead Nebula and beside it the Flame Nebula, both famous in photographs but far too faint to see by eye.
HR 15|Alpheratz|Alpha Andromedae, the princess's head and also a corner of the Great Square of Pegasus.|It was counted in both constellations, as Delta Pegasi too, until the constellations' borders were fixed in 1930.
HR 337|Mirach|Beta Andromedae, a red giant. From Mirach, hop up through two fainter stars to find the Andromeda Galaxy.|A faint galaxy, NGC 404, sits so close beside it that it is nicknamed Mirach's Ghost.
HR 2004|Saiph|Kappa Orionis, the hunter's other knee, a hot blue supergiant.|Its name means "the sword" in Arabic, though it is not part of Orion's sword.
HR 5563|Kochab|Beta Ursae Minoris, an orange giant at the end of the Little Dipper's bowl.|About 3,000 years ago Kochab was the bright star nearest the north celestial pole. With Pherkad it is still called a Guardian of the Pole.
HR 6556|Rasalhague|Alpha Ophiuchi, the head of the Serpent Bearer, a white star.|Its name means "head of the snake charmer" in Arabic.
HR 936|Algol|Beta Persei, the Demon Star, in the head of Medusa that Perseus carries. Every 2 days and 21 hours it fades by more than a magnitude for about 10 hours.|The dimming happens when a fainter star in orbit passes in front of the brighter one. John Goodricke, a deaf 18-year-old, suggested this in 1783.
HR 4534|Denebola|Beta Leonis, the tail of the Lion, a white star.|Denebola is surrounded by a disc of dust, which may be a sign of planets forming or already formed.
HR 7796|Sadr|Gamma Cygni, the centre of the Northern Cross, where the swan's wings meet its body.|Sadr means "the breast" in Arabic. It lies against the rich Cygnus star clouds, where binoculars show countless stars.
HR 168|Schedar|Alpha Cassiopeiae, an orange giant in the W of Cassiopeia.|Its name comes from the Arabic for "the breast", as it marks the queen's chest.
HR 1852|Mintaka|Delta Orionis, the western star of Orion's Belt.|Mintaka lies almost exactly on the celestial equator, so it rises due east and sets due west wherever you are on Earth.
HR 5793|Alphecca|Alpha Coronae Borealis, the bright jewel of the Northern Crown, also called Gemma.|Alphecca is an eclipsing pair: every 17 days one star passes in front of the other and it dims very slightly.
HR 6705|Eltanin|Gamma Draconis, the brightest star of Draco, an orange giant in the dragon's head.|While trying to measure Eltanin's distance in the 1720s, James Bradley discovered the aberration of starlight, the first direct proof that the Earth moves round the Sun.
HR 603|Almach|Gamma Andromedae, the princess's foot. A small telescope shows a lovely golden and blue pair.|It has at least four stars: the blue companion is itself a triple.
HR 21|Caph|Beta Cassiopeiae, a white star at one end of the W of Cassiopeia.|Caph lies almost on the line of 0 hours of right ascension, so it has long been used as a marker for where the sky's longitude starts.
HR 5054|Mizar|Zeta Ursae Majoris, the middle star of the Plough's handle. Sharp eyes see a fainter star, Alcor, beside it.|Mizar was the first double star seen through a telescope, in the 1600s, and the first binary found with a spectroscope, in 1889. With Alcor it is a family of six stars.
HR 5062|Alcor|The faint companion of Mizar in the Plough's handle.|Seeing Alcor beside Mizar was traditionally a test of good eyesight. In 2009 Alcor was found to have its own small red companion.
HR 4295|Merak|Beta Ursae Majoris, the bottom of the Plough's bowl. With Dubhe it makes the Pointers to Polaris.|Merak belongs to the Ursa Major Moving Group, a family of stars, including most of the Plough, that were born together and still travel together.
HR 8308|Enif|Epsilon Pegasi, an orange supergiant at the winged horse's nose.|Its name means "the nose". About 4° away is the globular cluster M15, a nice binocular target.
HR 8162|Alderamin|Alpha Cephei, a white star at the king's shoulder.|In about 5,500 years the wobble of the Earth's axis will make Alderamin the pole star.
HR 8781|Markab|Alpha Pegasi, a corner of the Great Square of Pegasus.|The Great Square is so big and empty that counting the stars you can see inside it is a classic test of how dark the sky is.
HR 681|Mira|Omicron Ceti, "the Wonderful", a pulsating red giant whose brightness swings over about 11 months, from easily visible to too faint to see without a telescope.|Mira was the first variable star (other than novae) to be recognised, by David Fabricius in 1596. It trails a tail of gas 13 light years long.
HR 1165|Alcyone|Eta Tauri, the brightest star of the Pleiades, a blue-white giant.|The Pleiades are named after seven sisters of Greek myth, Alcyone among them; their parents Atlas and Pleione have stars in the cluster too.
HR 5291|Thuban|Alpha Draconis, a white giant in the dragon's tail, not very bright despite being labelled alpha.|Thuban was the pole star when the Egyptian pyramids were built, about 4,500 years ago.
HR 5506|Izar|Epsilon Bootis, an orange giant with a close blue-green companion.|The pair is a fine sight in a telescope; the astronomer Friedrich Struve called it Pulcherrima, "the most beautiful".
HR 5531|Zubenelgenubi|Alpha Librae, a wide pair of white stars that sharp eyes can just split.|Its name means "the southern claw" in Arabic: the stars of Libra were once the claws of the neighbouring Scorpion.
HR 5685|Zubeneschamali|Beta Librae, "the northern claw", a blue-white star.|Some observers have reported that it looks greenish, which would make it the only green naked-eye star; most people see it as white.
HR 6406|Rasalgethi|Alpha Herculis, the head of Hercules, a red giant that varies in brightness.|A small telescope shows an orange star with a greenish companion, one of the sky's prettiest colour contrasts.
HR 7106|Sheliak|Beta Lyrae, a pair of stars so close together that gas streams from one to the other.|It is an eclipsing pair that dips every 12.9 days, enough to follow by eye: compare it with its neighbour Sulafat.
HR 7417|Albireo|Beta Cygni, the swan's head and the foot of the Northern Cross.|Even a small telescope shows a gold and blue pair, one of the most beautiful sights in the sky. Whether the two truly orbit each other is still debated.
HR 4915|Cor Caroli|Alpha Canum Venaticorum, named "Charles's Heart" after King Charles I of England.|It has an unusually strong magnetic field that gathers chemical elements into spots on its surface, the prototype of a whole class of variable stars.
HR 4932|Vindemiatrix|Epsilon Virginis, a yellow giant.|Its name means "the grape gatherer" in Latin: in ancient times its first appearance in the dawn sky marked the start of the grape harvest.
HR 8974|Errai|Gamma Cephei, an orange star with a known planet.|Its planet was first suspected in 1988, years before the first planets around Sun-like stars were confirmed. Around the year 4000 it will be the pole star.
HR 545|Mesarthim|Gamma Arietis, a pair of white stars that a small telescope splits easily.|It was one of the first double stars discovered, by Robert Hooke in 1664, by chance while he was following a comet.
HR 4057|Algieba|Gamma Leonis, in the Lion's mane, a pair of golden giants that a small telescope can split.|The Leonid meteor shower in November seems to come from a point close to it.
HR 4825|Porrima|Gamma Virginis, a pair of nearly identical white stars that circle each other every 169 years.|In 2005 the pair passed so close that no ordinary telescope could split them; they are now drawing apart again.
HR 897|Acamar|Theta Eridani, a pair of white stars in the river Eridanus.|Its name comes from the Arabic for "the end of the river": before Achernar was known to Mediterranean astronomers, Eridanus ended here.

# --- Messier objects ---
M1|Crab Nebula|The remains of a star that exploded in 1054, about 6,500 light years away. Chinese astronomers recorded the "guest star", bright enough to see in daylight for three weeks.|At its heart is a pulsar, a city-sized neutron star spinning 30 times a second. Messier began his catalogue after mistaking this nebula for a comet.
M2|M2|A globular cluster of about 150,000 stars, some 37,500 light years away, in Aquarius.|It is one of the larger globular clusters, about 175 light years across, and its stars are around 13 billion years old.
M3|M3|A globular cluster of about half a million stars, 34,000 light years away, in Canes Venatici.|M3 has more variable stars than any other known globular cluster, over 200 of them.
M4|M4|One of the nearest globular clusters, about 7,200 light years away, just west of Antares.|It contains one of the oldest known planets, about 12.7 billion years old and nicknamed Methuselah, orbiting a pulsar and a white dwarf.
M5|M5|A large, bright globular cluster about 24,500 light years away, in Serpens.|At around 13 billion years old, M5 is among the oldest globular clusters, almost as old as the universe.
M6|Butterfly Cluster|An open cluster of young stars about 1,600 light years away, near the Scorpion's sting. Binoculars show the shape of a butterfly with open wings.|Its brightest star, BM Scorpii, is an orange giant that glows warmly among its blue-white companions.
M7|Ptolemy Cluster|A large, bright open cluster about 1,000 light years away near the Scorpion's sting, easy to see with the naked eye.|Ptolemy described it as a nebula around 130 AD, one of the earliest deep-sky objects on record.
M8|Lagoon Nebula|A huge cloud of glowing hydrogen where stars are being born, about 4,100 light years away. From a dark site it is a misty patch to the naked eye, above the Teapot's spout.|A dark lane of dust across it gives the Lagoon its name. It holds small dark knots called Bok globules, collapsing clouds that may become stars.
M9|M9|A globular cluster about 26,000 light years away, near the centre of the galaxy.|It looks dimmer and redder than it should because thick clouds of the Milky Way's dust lie between it and us.
M10|M10|A bright globular cluster about 14,000 light years away, in Ophiuchus.|M10 and M12 are only a few degrees apart and make a fine pair for binoculars or a small telescope.
M11|Wild Duck Cluster|One of the richest and densest open clusters, nearly 3,000 stars about 6,000 light years away, in Scutum.|Its brightest stars form a V, like a flight of wild ducks, which gave it its name.
M12|M12|A loose globular cluster about 16,000 light years away, in Ophiuchus.|It seems to have lost many of its faint stars, pulled away by the Milky Way's gravity as it passed through the galaxy's disc.
M13|Hercules Globular Cluster|The finest globular cluster in the northern sky, several hundred thousand stars about 22,000 light years away. From a dark site it shows to the naked eye as a fuzzy star.|In 1974 the Arecibo radio telescope beamed a message towards M13. It will take about 25,000 years to get there.
M14|M14|A globular cluster about 30,000 light years away, in Ophiuchus.|A nova erupted in M14 in 1938, but no one noticed until it was found on old photographic plates in 1964.
M15|M15|One of the densest globular clusters, about 33,600 light years away, near the nose of Pegasus.|It was the first globular cluster found to contain a planetary nebula, Pease 1, in 1928. Its core has collapsed, packing its stars extremely tightly.
M16|Eagle Nebula|A young star cluster in a cloud of glowing gas about 7,000 light years away, in Serpens.|Hubble's famous photograph "Pillars of Creation" shows towers of dust at its heart, each light years tall, where new stars are forming.
M17|Omega Nebula|One of the brightest star-forming nebulae in the sky, about 5,500 light years away in Sagittarius.|It goes by many names, the Omega, Swan, Horseshoe and Lobster Nebula, depending on what people saw in its shape.
M18|M18|A small open cluster of young stars about 4,900 light years away, between the Omega Nebula and the Sagittarius Star Cloud.|It is often overlooked, because its neighbours M17 and M24 are so much more spectacular.
M19|M19|A globular cluster about 28,700 light years away, close to the centre of the galaxy.|It is one of the most flattened globular clusters known, noticeably oval in a telescope.
M20|Trifid Nebula|A nebula about 5,200 light years away whose glow is split into three by dark lanes of dust, hence "Trifid".|It has all three kinds of nebula together: a red glowing nebula, a blue reflection nebula and dark lanes of dust.
M21|M21|A young open cluster about 4,000 light years away, just north-east of the Trifid Nebula.|Its stars are only about 5 million years old, babies by cosmic standards.
M22|Sagittarius Cluster|One of the brightest globular clusters, about 10,600 light years away, near the lid of the Teapot.|It was probably the first globular cluster ever discovered, by Abraham Ihle in 1665. It also holds a planetary nebula, a rarity in such clusters.
M23|M23|An open cluster of about 150 stars, some 2,100 light years away, in Sagittarius.|At around 300 million years old, it is a mature cluster among the young ones in this part of the sky.
M24|Sagittarius Star Cloud|Not a cluster but a window: a gap in the Milky Way's dust shows a cloud of countless distant stars, 10,000 light years away and more.|Messier counted it as a single object; at about 1.5° across it is one of the largest in his catalogue.
M25|M25|A bright open cluster about 2,000 light years away, easy in binoculars.|It contains U Sagittarii, a Cepheid variable star whose regular pulsing helps astronomers measure distances.
M26|M26|A small open cluster about 5,000 light years away, in Scutum.|Its centre looks oddly sparse in stars, perhaps because a cloud of dust lies in front of it.
M27|Dumbbell Nebula|The first planetary nebula discovered, by Messier in 1764: the cast-off outer layers of a dying star, about 1,300 light years away.|Its shape is like an apple core or a dumbbell. The white dwarf at its centre, the star's leftover core, has a surface hotter than 80,000 °C.
M28|M28|A globular cluster about 18,000 light years away, near the lid of the Teapot.|In 1987 it was the first globular cluster found to contain a millisecond pulsar, a dead star spinning over 300 times a second.
M29|M29|A small open cluster of young hot stars about 4,000 light years away, near Sadr in Cygnus.|Its stars would look about fifteen times brighter if the Milky Way's dust were not in the way.
M30|M30|A globular cluster about 27,000 light years away, in Capricornus.|Its core has collapsed, crowding its stars so tightly that close encounters between them are common.
M31|Andromeda Galaxy|The nearest large galaxy to our own, a spiral of about a trillion stars some 2.5 million light years away, and the most distant thing most people can see with the naked eye.|Andromeda and the Milky Way are heading towards each other and may merge in about 5 billion years. Its full extent in the sky is about six times the width of the full Moon.
M32|M32|A small elliptical galaxy that orbits the Andromeda Galaxy, about 2.5 million light years away.|It is unusually compact, perhaps the core left over after Andromeda stripped away most of its outer stars.
M33|Triangulum Galaxy|The third-largest galaxy of our Local Group, a face-on spiral about 2.7 million light years away.|From a very dark site some people can just see it without optical aid, which would make it the most distant thing visible to the naked eye.
M34|M34|An open cluster of about 100 stars, some 1,500 light years away, in Perseus.|It is a fine binocular target, about the width of the full Moon, halfway between Algol and Almach.
M35|M35|A rich open cluster about 2,800 light years away, at the feet of Gemini.|A telescope shows a second, much more distant cluster, NGC 2158, in the same view, four or five times farther away.
M36|M36|A young open cluster about 4,100 light years away, one of three Messier clusters in Auriga.|M36, M37 and M38 lie roughly in a line and can all be seen in one sweep with binoculars.
M37|M37|The richest of the three Auriga clusters, about 500 stars some 4,500 light years away.|It holds about a dozen red giants that stand out against its blue-white stars.
M38|M38|An open cluster about 4,200 light years away in Auriga; some see a cross or the Greek letter pi in its stars.|A smaller, more distant cluster, NGC 1907, sits just beside it.
M39|M39|A large, scattered open cluster about 800 light years away in Cygnus.|It is about as wide as the full Moon, so it looks better in binoculars than in most telescopes.
M40|Winnecke 4|Not a nebula at all but a pair of unrelated stars in Ursa Major.|Messier was looking for a nebula that Hevelius had reported and found only this double star, one of the oddest entries in his catalogue.
M41|M41|A bright open cluster about 2,300 light years away, just south of Sirius, visible to the naked eye from a dark site.|Aristotle may have mentioned it around 325 BC, which would make it the faintest object recorded in classical antiquity.
M42|Orion Nebula|The brightest star-forming nebula in the sky, about 1,350 light years away. It is the misty "star" in the middle of Orion's sword, easy to see with the naked eye.|At its heart is the Trapezium, four young stars whose light makes the gas glow. Hubble has found dozens of dusty discs round young stars here, planetary systems in the making.
M43|De Mairan's Nebula|A small part of the Orion Nebula complex, separated from it by a lane of dark dust.|Its glow comes mainly from a single young star, which is why it looks like a comma.
M44|Beehive Cluster|One of the nearest open clusters, about 600 light years away: a misty patch to the eye in Cancer and a fine sight in binoculars.|Galileo was the first to see it as a swarm of stars, counting about 40, in 1609-10. Planets have been found around several of its stars.
M45|Pleiades|The Seven Sisters, the best-known star cluster, about 440 light years away. It holds over 1,000 young stars, born about 100 million years ago.|Most people see six stars, sharp eyes under dark skies nine or more. The blue haze in photographs is dust the cluster is passing through, not the cloud it was born from.
M46|M46|A rich open cluster of hundreds of stars about 5,000 light years away, in Puppis.|A planetary nebula, NGC 2438, appears inside it, but only by chance: the nebula is probably much nearer to us.
M47|M47|A bright, scattered open cluster about 1,600 light years away, next to M46.|Messier recorded its position wrongly, and it was lost until 1959, when T. F. Morris worked out his mistake.
M48|M48|A large open cluster about 1,500 light years away, in Hydra.|Messier's position for it was off by several degrees, so for many years it was counted as "missing".
M49|M49|A giant elliptical galaxy in the Virgo Cluster, about 56 million light years away.|It was the first member of the Virgo Cluster of galaxies to be discovered, by Messier in 1771.
M50|M50|An open cluster about 3,000 light years away, in Monoceros.|You can find it about a third of the way from Sirius to Procyon.
M51|Whirlpool Galaxy|A face-on spiral galaxy about 25 million light years away, with a smaller companion galaxy at the end of one arm.|In 1845 Lord Rosse drew its arms through his giant telescope, and it became the first "nebula" known to have a spiral shape.
M52|M52|A rich open cluster about 5,000 light years away, in Cassiopeia.|Nearby is the Bubble Nebula, a shell of gas blown out by a single hot, massive star.
M53|M53|A globular cluster about 58,000 light years away, in Coma Berenices.|It lies far out in the halo of the galaxy, and its stars are poor in heavy elements, a sign of great age.
M54|M54|A dense globular cluster that seems to be in Sagittarius but is about 87,000 light years away.|It belongs to the Sagittarius Dwarf Galaxy, which the Milky Way is tearing apart: the first globular cluster found outside our galaxy, though no one knew it at the time.
M55|M55|A large, loose globular cluster about 17,600 light years away, in Sagittarius.|Lacaille found it in 1752 from South Africa. Messier, in France, struggled to see it because it stays low in his sky.
M56|M56|A small globular cluster about 33,000 light years away, between Albireo and Sulafat.|It orbits the Milky Way backwards, so it may have been captured from a dwarf galaxy.
M57|Ring Nebula|A planetary nebula about 2,300 light years away: a shell of gas thrown off by a dying star, seen almost end-on as a ring.|It lies between the two bottom stars of Lyra's parallelogram and looks like a tiny smoke ring in a small telescope.
M58|M58|A barred spiral galaxy in the Virgo Cluster, about 62 million light years away.|Two supernovae have been seen in it, in 1988 and 1989.
M59|M59|An elliptical galaxy in the Virgo Cluster, about 60 million light years away.|It is less than half a degree from M60, so both fit in one telescope view.
M60|M60|A giant elliptical galaxy in the Virgo Cluster, about 55 million light years away.|At its centre is a black hole of about 4.5 billion Suns, one of the most massive known. A spiral galaxy, NGC 4647, seems to touch its edge.
M61|M61|A face-on barred spiral galaxy in the Virgo Cluster, about 52 million light years away.|It is one of the busiest galaxies for supernovae: at least eight have been seen in it since 1926.
M62|M62|A globular cluster about 22,000 light years away, close to the centre of the galaxy.|It is one of the most lopsided globular clusters, pulled out of shape by the centre of the galaxy.
M63|Sunflower Galaxy|A spiral galaxy about 29 million light years away, in Canes Venatici.|Its many short, patchy arms give it the look of a sunflower head.
M64|Black Eye Galaxy|A spiral galaxy about 17 million light years away, with a dark band of dust across its bright centre.|Its outer gas turns in the opposite direction to its inner stars, probably because it swallowed a smaller galaxy long ago.
M65|M65|A spiral galaxy about 35 million light years away, one of the Leo Triplet with M66 and NGC 3628.|All three galaxies of the Leo Triplet fit in one view of a small telescope at low power.
M66|M66|The brightest of the Leo Triplet of galaxies, a spiral about 36 million light years away.|Its arms look lopsided after a close encounter with its neighbour NGC 3628.
M67|M67|One of the oldest open clusters known, about 4 billion years old and 2,700 light years away, in Cancer.|Its stars are about as old as the Sun and of similar make-up, so astronomers have searched it for the Sun's long-lost siblings.
M68|M68|A globular cluster about 33,000 light years away, in Hydra.|It is one of the globular clusters poorest in heavy elements, a sign that it formed very early, and it may have been captured from a dwarf galaxy.
M69|M69|A globular cluster about 29,700 light years away, near the base of the Teapot.|Unusually for a globular cluster, its stars are fairly rich in heavier elements.
M70|M70|A small globular cluster about 29,000 light years away, beside M69 at the base of the Teapot.|Comet Hale-Bopp, the great comet of 1997, was discovered right next to M70 in July 1995.
M71|M71|A loose globular cluster about 13,000 light years away, in the little constellation Sagitta.|For decades astronomers argued whether it was a dense open cluster or a sparse globular one; studies of its stars settled on globular.
M72|M72|A faint, distant globular cluster about 55,000 light years away, in Aquarius.|It is one of the most distant globular clusters in Messier's list, a small fuzzy patch even in a telescope.
M73|M73|Just four faint stars in a little Y shape, in Aquarius.|Messier listed it as a cluster. Studies in 2002 showed the four stars are at different distances and only happen to line up.
M74|Phantom Galaxy|A face-on spiral galaxy about 32 million light years away, in Pisces.|Its light is spread so thinly that it is one of the hardest Messier objects to see, hence the nickname.
M75|M75|A distant globular cluster about 67,000 light years away, in Sagittarius.|It is one of the most densely packed globular clusters known.
M76|Little Dumbbell Nebula|A planetary nebula about 2,500 light years away in Perseus, one of the faintest objects in Messier's list.|It looks like a small version of the Dumbbell Nebula, M27, which gave it its name; it is also called the Cork Nebula.
M77|M77|A barred spiral galaxy about 47 million light years away, in Cetus.|It has an extremely bright, active centre, powered by a feeding black hole: one of the first Seyfert galaxies known.
M78|M78|The brightest reflection nebula in the sky: dust lit by young stars about 1,350 light years away, in Orion.|Unlike the glowing gas of the Orion Nebula, M78 shines by starlight reflected off dust, which is why it looks blue in photographs.
M79|M79|A globular cluster about 41,000 light years away, in Lepus, in a part of the sky where few globulars are found.|It may not be ours: it might have come from the Canis Major Dwarf Galaxy, which is being absorbed by the Milky Way.
M80|M80|One of the densest globular clusters, about 32,600 light years away, in Scorpius.|In 1860 a nova, T Scorpii, flared up in it and briefly outshone the whole cluster.
M81|Bode's Galaxy|A grand spiral galaxy about 12 million light years away, one of the brightest galaxies in the sky.|Its neighbour M82 is half a degree away. Their last close pass set off a burst of star formation in M82.
M82|Cigar Galaxy|A starburst galaxy about 12 million light years away, seen edge-on beside M81.|It is forming stars about ten times faster than the Milky Way, and winds from them blow great plumes of gas out of the galaxy.
M83|Southern Pinwheel Galaxy|A barred spiral galaxy about 15 million light years away, in Hydra, one of the nearest and brightest of its kind.|Six supernovae have been seen in it in the last hundred years, among the most of any galaxy.
M84|M84|A lens-shaped galaxy in the heart of the Virgo Cluster, about 60 million light years away.|It is at one end of Markarian's Chain, a curved line of galaxies that can be followed across the Virgo Cluster in a telescope.
M85|M85|A lens-shaped galaxy about 60 million light years away, the most northerly member of the Virgo Cluster.|Its light set out about 60 million years ago, not long after the dinosaurs died out.
M86|M86|A lens-shaped galaxy in the Virgo Cluster, about 52 million light years away.|It is one of the few galaxies moving towards us: it is falling through the cluster at about 240 km/s in our direction.
M87|Virgo A|A giant elliptical galaxy at the heart of the Virgo Cluster, about 54 million light years away, with thousands of globular clusters.|In 2019 the black hole at its centre, 6.5 billion times the Sun's mass, became the first ever imaged. It shoots out a jet of matter thousands of light years long.
M88|M88|A spiral galaxy in the Virgo Cluster, about 50 million light years away.|It is moving away from us at over 2,000 km/s, one of the fastest of the cluster's galaxies.
M89|M89|An almost perfectly round elliptical galaxy in the Virgo Cluster, about 50 million light years away.|It is surrounded by faint shells and plumes of stars, the debris of smaller galaxies it has swallowed.
M90|M90|A spiral galaxy in the Virgo Cluster, about 60 million light years away.|Falling through the cluster has stripped away much of its gas, so it has almost stopped forming new stars.
M91|M91|A barred spiral galaxy in Coma Berenices, about 60 million light years away.|It was "missing" for nearly two centuries because Messier wrote down the wrong position; in 1969 William Williams worked out that he meant NGC 4548.
M92|M92|A bright globular cluster about 27,000 light years away in Hercules, often overshadowed by its neighbour M13.|It is among the oldest known globular clusters, with stars over 12 billion years old.
M93|M93|A bright open cluster about 3,600 light years away, in Puppis.|It is often said to be the last object that Messier discovered himself, in 1781.
M94|Cat's Eye Galaxy|A spiral galaxy about 16 million light years away, with a bright ring of new stars around its centre.|The ring of young stars round its core gives it the look of an eye, hence its nickname.
M95|M95|A barred spiral galaxy about 33 million light years away, in Leo.|In 2012 a supernova, SN 2012aw, flared up in one of its arms.
M96|M96|The brightest galaxy of the Leo I group, a spiral about 31 million light years away.|It is lopsided, its arms and core pulled out of shape by its neighbours.
M97|Owl Nebula|A planetary nebula about 2,000 light years away, in Ursa Major.|Two dark patches in its round disc look like an owl's eyes, which gave it its name in 1848.
M98|M98|A spiral galaxy seen nearly edge-on, about 45 million light years away, on the edge of the Virgo Cluster.|It lies half a degree from the star 6 Comae Berenices, which helps you find it.
M99|Coma Pinwheel|A face-on spiral galaxy in the Virgo Cluster, about 50 million light years away.|It was one of the first galaxies whose spiral shape was seen, drawn by Lord Rosse in 1846. One of its arms is stretched, probably by a passing encounter.
M100|M100|A grand spiral galaxy in the Virgo Cluster, about 55 million light years away.|In 1993 the newly repaired Hubble Space Telescope took a famous picture of M100 to show off its new, sharp sight.
M101|Pinwheel Galaxy|A large face-on spiral galaxy about 21 million light years away, near the Plough's handle.|Two bright supernovae have been seen in it recently: SN 2011fe, the closest of its type in decades, and SN 2023ixf.
M102|Spindle Galaxy|A lens-shaped galaxy seen edge-on, about 50 million light years away, in Draco.|Which object Messier meant by M102 has been argued over for two centuries; most now take it to be this galaxy, NGC 5866.
M103|M103|A small open cluster of a few dozen stars about 9,000 light years away, in Cassiopeia.|It was the last object in Messier's catalogue as he published it in 1781; M104 to M110 were added later from his and Méchain's notes.
M104|Sombrero Galaxy|A spiral galaxy about 31 million light years away, seen nearly edge-on, with a bright bulge and a dark lane of dust like the brim of a hat.|It has around 2,000 globular clusters, about ten times as many as the Milky Way.
M105|M105|An elliptical galaxy about 32 million light years away, in Leo.|Its central black hole weighs about 200 million Suns.
M106|M106|A spiral galaxy about 24 million light years away, in Canes Venatici.|Water molecules near its central black hole act as natural masers, which let astronomers measure its distance precisely, a key rung on the cosmic distance ladder.
M107|M107|A loose globular cluster about 21,000 light years away, in Ophiuchus.|It was added to the Messier list in 1947, by Helen Sawyer Hogg, from Méchain's notes.
M108|Surfboard Galaxy|An edge-on spiral galaxy about 46 million light years away, close to the Owl Nebula.|It and the Owl Nebula, M97, sit less than a degree apart, but the galaxy is over 20,000 times farther away.
M109|M109|A barred spiral galaxy about 67 million light years away, near Phecda in the Plough's bowl.|Like the Milky Way it has a bar of stars across its middle, which makes it a useful comparison for our own galaxy.
M110|M110|A dwarf elliptical galaxy, a satellite of the Andromeda Galaxy, about 2.7 million light years away.|It was the last object added to the Messier catalogue, in 1966, though Messier himself had drawn it beside M31 in 1773.

# --- Other galaxies ---
LMC|Large Magellanic Cloud|A satellite galaxy of the Milky Way, about 160,000 light years away. To the naked eye it looks like a detached piece of the Milky Way in the southern sky.|In 1987 a star in it exploded as supernova 1987A, the closest supernova seen since 1604. The cloud is named after Ferdinand Magellan, whose crew described it.
SMC|Small Magellanic Cloud|A dwarf galaxy and satellite of the Milky Way, about 200,000 light years away, in the far southern sky.|Henrietta Leavitt's study of variable stars in it led to the period-luminosity law of 1912, which first let astronomers measure distances to other galaxies.
NGC 253|Sculptor Galaxy|A dusty spiral galaxy about 11 million light years away, one of the brightest galaxies beyond our Local Group.|It is a starburst galaxy, forming stars at a furious rate. Caroline Herschel discovered it in 1783.
NGC 5128|Centaurus A|A large galaxy about 12 million light years away, with a dark band of dust across a bright elliptical glow.|It is one of the strongest radio sources in the sky: a black hole of about 55 million Suns at its centre fires jets of particles a million light years long.
NGC 55|NGC 55|An edge-on galaxy about 7 million light years away, in Sculptor.|It is one of the nearest galaxies beyond our Local Group and may be a larger cousin of the Magellanic Clouds, seen side-on.
NGC 300|NGC 300|A face-on spiral galaxy about 6 million light years away, in Sculptor.|It looks much like M33, the Triangulum Galaxy, and the two are often compared.
NGC 247|NGC 247|A spiral galaxy about 11 million light years away, in Cetus.|A large empty stretch along one side, nicknamed the Needle's Eye, gives it a lopsided look.
NGC 4945|NGC 4945|An edge-on spiral galaxy about 12 million light years away, in Centaurus.|Its centre hides an active black hole behind thick dust; it is one of the brightest galaxies in high-energy X-rays.
NGC 6744|NGC 6744|A large spiral galaxy about 30 million light years away, in Pavo.|It is often called one of the galaxies most like our own Milky Way, though it is about twice as wide.
NGC 2403|NGC 2403|A spiral galaxy about 10 million light years away, in Camelopardalis.|It was the first galaxy beyond our Local Group in which Cepheid variable stars were found, which helped to measure its distance.
IC 342|Hidden Galaxy|A large face-on spiral galaxy about 11 million light years away, close to the plane of the Milky Way.|Without the Milky Way's dust in the way, it would be one of the brightest galaxies in the sky; the dust hides so much of it that it is called the Hidden Galaxy.
NGC 6822|Barnard's Galaxy|A small irregular galaxy of our Local Group, about 1.6 million light years away, in Sagittarius.|In 1925 Edwin Hubble used its variable stars to show it lies far beyond the Milky Way, among the first proofs that other galaxies exist.
NGC 4565|Needle Galaxy|One of the finest edge-on spiral galaxies, about 40 million light years away, in Coma Berenices.|Its thin, dusty disc is so long and straight that it looks like a needle, with a small bulge at its middle.
NGC 891|NGC 891|An edge-on spiral galaxy about 30 million light years away, in Andromeda.|It is thought to look much as our own Milky Way would if seen side-on from far away.
NGC 7331|NGC 7331|A spiral galaxy about 40 million light years away, in Pegasus.|It is often called the Milky Way's twin for its similar size and shape. Nearby lies Stephan's Quintet, a tight group of galaxies.
NGC 4631|Whale Galaxy|An edge-on spiral galaxy about 25 million light years away, in Canes Venatici, slightly bent like the body of a whale.|A small companion galaxy, the "calf", sits just beside it.
NGC 6946|Fireworks Galaxy|A face-on spiral galaxy about 25 million light years away, on the border of Cepheus and Cygnus.|It earned its nickname with ten supernovae in a century, more than any other galaxy known.
NGC 2903|NGC 2903|A barred spiral galaxy about 30 million light years away, in Leo.|Messier never listed it, though it is brighter than many galaxies he did; William Herschel found it in 1784.
NGC 3115|NGC 3115|A lens-shaped galaxy seen edge-on, about 32 million light years away, in Sextans; also called the Spindle Galaxy.|At its centre is a black hole of about a billion Suns, one of the nearest of that size.
NGC 1097|NGC 1097|A barred spiral galaxy about 45 million light years away, in Fornax.|A bright ring of star formation circles its active centre, and faint streams of stars stretch far out from it.

# --- Globular clusters ---
NGC 5139|Omega Centauri|The largest and brightest globular cluster in the sky, about 10 million stars some 17,000 light years away, easily seen with the naked eye.|It may be the core of a small galaxy that the Milky Way swallowed. Ptolemy listed it as a star, which is how it got the Greek letter omega.
NGC 104|47 Tucanae|The second-brightest globular cluster, about 15,000 light years away, beside the Small Magellanic Cloud.|It contains at least two dozen millisecond pulsars, dead stars spinning hundreds of times a second.
NGC 6397|NGC 6397|One of the two nearest globular clusters, about 7,800 light years away, in Ara.|Hubble found its faintest white dwarfs, which cool like embers and give the cluster an age of about 12 billion years.
NGC 6752|Great Peacock Globular|A bright globular cluster about 13,000 light years away, in Pavo.|It is the third-brightest globular cluster in the sky, after Omega Centauri and 47 Tucanae.
NGC 362|NGC 362|A globular cluster about 30,000 light years away that appears beside the Small Magellanic Cloud.|It is not part of the cloud: it belongs to the Milky Way and is about seven times nearer.
NGC 3201|NGC 3201|A loose globular cluster about 16,000 light years away, in Vela.|It is one of the few globular clusters that orbit the Milky Way backwards, so it may have been captured.
NGC 2808|NGC 2808|A massive globular cluster about 31,000 light years away, in Carina.|It holds at least five generations of stars, which upset the old idea that each globular cluster formed in a single burst.
NGC 1851|NGC 1851|A dense globular cluster about 39,000 light years away, in Columba.|It holds at least two distinct populations of stars, a sign that it formed in more than one burst or from a merger.

# --- Open clusters and asterisms ---
Mel 25|Hyades|The nearest open cluster to the Sun, about 150 light years away: the V of stars that forms the Bull's face.|Bright Aldebaran looks like a member but lies in front, less than half as far away. In Greek myth the Hyades were half-sisters of the Pleiades.
NGC 869|Double Cluster (h Persei)|One half of the Double Cluster, two rich open clusters side by side about 7,500 light years away in Perseus.|The pair shows to the naked eye as a hazy patch and is superb in binoculars. Its stars are only about 13 million years old.
NGC 884|Double Cluster (chi Persei)|The other half of the Double Cluster, about 7,500 light years away in Perseus.|It has several red supergiants among its blue-white stars, which show as orange points in a telescope.

Mel 111|Coma Star Cluster|A large scattered open cluster about 280 light years away, which makes up most of the constellation Coma Berenices.|It is one of the nearest open clusters to the Sun. Its stars are said to be the hair of Queen Berenice.
Cr 399|Coathanger|A group of ten stars that makes an almost perfect coat hanger, in Vulpecula; also called Brocchi's Cluster.|It is not a real cluster: its stars are at very different distances and only happen to line up.
NGC 2244|Rosette Cluster|A young open cluster about 5,000 light years away, at the centre of the Rosette Nebula.|Its hot young stars, only a few million years old, are blowing out a hole in the middle of the nebula with their light and winds.
NGC 457|Owl Cluster|An open cluster about 8,000 light years away in Cassiopeia, also called the ET Cluster.|Its two brightest stars look like an owl's eyes, or ET's, with the other stars making the body and outstretched wings.
NGC 752|NGC 752|A large, old open cluster about 1,500 light years away, in Andromeda.|At over a billion years old, it is unusually long-lived for an open cluster.
NGC 6231|NGC 6231|A bright young open cluster about 5,000 light years away, near the Scorpion's tail, sometimes called the Northern Jewel Box.|With Zeta Scorpii and the stars around it, it forms the "False Comet", which looks like a comet's head and tail to the naked eye.
NGC 3532|Wishing Well Cluster|A large, bright open cluster about 1,300 light years away, in Carina.|It was the first target observed by the Hubble Space Telescope, in 1990. Its glittering stars look like coins at the bottom of a well.
NGC 2516|Southern Beehive Cluster|A bright open cluster about 1,300 light years away, in Carina, visible to the naked eye.|It looks much like the Beehive Cluster in Cancer, which gave it its nickname.
IC 2602|Southern Pleiades|A bright open cluster about 480 light years away, in Carina, with the star Theta Carinae among its members.|It is easy to see with the naked eye and looks like a smaller Pleiades, hence its name.
IC 2391|Omicron Velorum Cluster|A bright open cluster about 500 light years away, in Vela.|It was first recorded by the Persian astronomer al-Sufi around 964 AD.
NGC 4755|Jewel Box|A small, bright open cluster about 6,400 light years away, beside Mimosa in the Southern Cross.|John Herschel likened it to "a casket of variously coloured precious stones": bright blue-white stars round a striking orange-red supergiant.
NGC 2362|Tau Canis Majoris Cluster|A small young open cluster about 4,800 light years away, around the bright star Tau Canis Majoris.|At about 5 million years old it is one of the youngest clusters known; Tau CMa, a massive multiple star, outshines all the rest.
NGC 6633|NGC 6633|A loose open cluster about 1,200 light years away, in Ophiuchus.|It is a fine binocular sight, and a few degrees away lies a similar cluster, IC 4756, in Serpens.
IC 4756|IC 4756|A large, scattered open cluster about 1,600 light years away, in Serpens.|With its neighbour NGC 6633 it makes a pair sometimes nicknamed Tweedledum and Tweedledee.
NGC 7243|NGC 7243|An open cluster about 2,800 light years away, in Lacerta.|Lacerta is a dim constellation, and this cluster is its best sight for binoculars.
NGC 2264|Christmas Tree Cluster|A young cluster about 2,500 light years away, in Monoceros, whose stars outline a tree.|At the tree's tip is the dark Cone Nebula, and at its base the bright star S Monocerotis.
NGC 6025|NGC 6025|An open cluster about 2,700 light years away, in Triangulum Australe.|Lacaille found it in 1751, while charting the southern sky from the Cape of Good Hope.
NGC 3766|Pearl Cluster|A rich open cluster about 5,800 light years away, in Centaurus.|Lacaille discovered it in 1751; its bright stars are strung across it like pearls.
NGC 6124|NGC 6124|An open cluster about 1,800 light years away, in Scorpius.|Lacaille discovered it in 1751, one of many clusters he found from the Cape of Good Hope.
NGC 6193|NGC 6193|A young open cluster about 3,600 light years away, in Ara.|Its hottest stars light up and sculpt the clouds of NGC 6188 beside it, known in photographs as the Fighting Dragons of Ara.
NGC 2477|NGC 2477|A rich open cluster of about 300 stars, some 3,700 light years away, in Puppis.|It is unusually old for an open cluster, around a billion years.

# --- Nebulae ---
NGC 3372|Carina Nebula|One of the largest and brightest nebulae in the sky, about 7,500 light years away, easily seen with the naked eye in the southern sky.|It contains Eta Carinae, a massive unstable star system that flared up in 1843 to become the second-brightest star in the night sky.
NGC 7000|North America Nebula|A large cloud of glowing hydrogen near Deneb, about 2,600 light years away, shaped like the North American continent.|It is about four times the width of the full Moon but very faint; it shows best in long-exposure photographs.
NGC 6992|Eastern Veil Nebula|Part of the Cygnus Loop, the expanding remains of a star that exploded perhaps 10,000 to 20,000 years ago, about 2,400 light years away.|The whole loop is about six times the width of the full Moon, and its shock wave is still spreading at hundreds of km/s.
NGC 6960|Western Veil Nebula|The western arc of the Cygnus Loop, the remains of an exploded star about 2,400 light years away. It passes right behind the star 52 Cygni.|It is sometimes called the Witch's Broom, with 52 Cygni near the end of the handle.
NGC 2237|Rosette Nebula|A large round cloud of glowing gas around the Rosette Cluster, about 5,000 light years away.|It is about 130 light years across, with enough gas to make about 10,000 Suns.
NGC 2070|Tarantula Nebula|A huge star-forming nebula in the Large Magellanic Cloud, about 160,000 light years away.|If it were as close as the Orion Nebula it would cast shadows. It contains R136a1, one of the most massive stars known.
NGC 7293|Helix Nebula|One of the nearest planetary nebulae, about 650 light years away, in Aquarius.|It is sometimes called the Eye of God, from its look in photographs. It is about half the width of the full Moon, but faint.
NGC 6543|Cat's Eye Nebula|A planetary nebula about 3,300 light years away, in Draco.|It was the first nebula whose spectrum was examined, by William Huggins in 1864, which showed it was glowing gas rather than stars.
NGC 2392|Eskimo Nebula|A planetary nebula about 4,000 light years away, in Gemini.|In a telescope it looks like a face in a fur hood, hence the names Eskimo or Clown Face Nebula.
NGC 7009|Saturn Nebula|A planetary nebula about 4,000 light years away, in Aquarius.|Two faint extensions look like Saturn's rings seen edge-on, which is how Lord Rosse came to name it.
NGC 3242|Ghost of Jupiter|A planetary nebula about 4,000 light years away, in Hydra.|In a telescope it looks about the same size as Jupiter, hence its name.
NGC 6826|Blinking Planetary|A planetary nebula about 2,200 light years away, in Cygnus.|Look straight at it and the nebula fades, leaving its star; look to one side and the nebula comes back, so it seems to blink.
C 99|Coalsack Nebula|A dark cloud of dust about 600 light years away that blots out the Milky Way beside the Southern Cross.|It is the most obvious dark nebula to the naked eye. In Aboriginal astronomy it is the head of the Emu in the Sky.
